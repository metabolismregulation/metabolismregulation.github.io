## Description

The hexosamine pathway turns a small part of the glucose that enters a cell into UDP-N-acetylglucosamine, the donor sugar for glycosylation and for the O-GlcNAc modification of nuclear and cytoplasmic proteins. Its first and rate-limiting step transfers the amide nitrogen of glutamine to fructose 6-phosphate, giving glucosamine 6-phosphate, and acetylation, isomerisation and uridylylation then complete the sugar. Because the step needs a sugar phosphate, an amino acid, acetyl-CoA and a nucleotide, the pathway reads the supply of all four. The end product controls the entry: UDP-N-acetylglucosamine binds the first enzyme and inhibits it with an inhibition constant in the low micromolar range, and the structure shows that it acts through the linker between the two domains (Broschat 2002, Ruegenberg 2020). Two kinases write onto the same enzyme. AMP-activated protein kinase phosphorylates Ser261 when energy or glucose runs short, which lowers the activity and the O-GlcNAc level of primary human endothelial cells (Eguchi 2009, Zibrova 2017). Protein kinase A phosphorylates Ser205, and structure-guided work shows that this lowers the basal rate and at the same time takes the enzyme out of the feedback loop, so that cyclic AMP changes not the rate alone but the logic of the pathway (Chang 2000, Ruegenberg 2021). How much enzyme a cell holds is controlled as well, by the unfolded protein response and by amino acid shortage (Wang 2014, Chaveroux 2016). Loss of the enzyme causes a congenital myasthenic syndrome with tubular aggregates, which shows how much the neuromuscular junction depends on this flux (Senderek 2011).
