## Description

In the liver, glucokinase sets the rate at which hepatocytes commit glucose to storage and glycolysis by phosphorylating it to glucose 6-phosphate. Its activity is governed by the glucokinase regulatory protein, which binds glucokinase into an inactive complex and holds it in the nucleus when glucose is scarce (Van Schaftingen 1989, Shiota 1999, de la Iglesia 1999). Fructose 6-phosphate, a signal of ample hexose phosphate, strengthens this complex, whereas fructose 1-phosphate, formed from dietary fructose, competes for the same site on the regulatory protein and frees the enzyme (Vandercammen 1992, Veiga-da-Cunha 2002, Pautsch 2013). A rise in glucose likewise dissociates the complex, and glucokinase then leaves the nucleus through a signal in its own sequence while the regulatory protein stays behind, so the enzyme is placed in the cytoplasm when glucose must be used (Shiota 1999, Agius 1993). Glucokinase has no nuclear localisation signal of its own and depends on the regulatory protein to enter the nucleus. In the cytoplasm it also associates with the bifunctional enzyme 6-phosphofructo-2-kinase and fructose-2,6-bisphosphatase, which stabilises and activates it (Baltrusch 2001, Smith 2007). A common variant of the regulatory protein, P446L, blunts its response to fructose 6-phosphate and raises cytosolic glucokinase activity, linking this control point to plasma triglyceride and glucose levels (Beer 2009). Small molecules that disrupt the complex or activate the enzyme have been developed from the same mechanism (Lloyd 2013).
