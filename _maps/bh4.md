## Description

Tetrahydrobiopterin (BH4) is the cofactor of the aromatic amino acid hydroxylases and of the nitric oxide synthases, and cells make it in three steps from GTP (Thöny 2000). GTP cyclohydrolase I (GCH1) opens the purine ring to 7,8-dihydroneopterin triphosphate (H2NTP) and releases formate. 6-Pyruvoyltetrahydropterin synthase (PTPS) removes the triphosphate to give 6-pyruvoyl-tetrahydropterin (6-PTP), and sepiapterin reductase (SPR) completes BH4 with two molecules of NADPH. The first step sets the rate of the pathway and is the one the cell controls. Its control runs through a small partner protein, the GTP cyclohydrolase I feedback regulatory protein (GFRP), which binds the cyclohydrolase only together with a ligand (Harada 1993, Yoneyama 1998). With the end product BH4 the two proteins form a complex in which the active site is held in an inactive conformation, so the pathway shuts itself down (Maita 2004). With L-phenylalanine they form a complex of the same architecture that keeps the enzyme active, so a rising load of phenylalanine calls for more cofactor (Maita 2002). How much this brake matters in cells is debated, since the amount of GCH1 sets BH4 levels in several cell types (Tatham 2009), while GFRP limits the rise of BH4 after inflammatory stimulation (Nandi 2008). Phosphorylation adds a second layer. In endothelial cells laminar flow has casein kinase 2 phosphorylate GCH1 on Ser81, which raises its activity and weakens its binding to GFRP (Widder 2007, Li 2010). cGMP-dependent protein kinase II phosphorylates PTPS on Ser19, which the enzyme needs for its full activity in cells (Scherer-Oppliger 1999). Because an uncoupled nitric oxide synthase makes superoxide instead of nitric oxide, the amount of BH4 relative to its oxidised form decides whether the endothelium produces a signal or an oxidant (Crabtree 2009).
